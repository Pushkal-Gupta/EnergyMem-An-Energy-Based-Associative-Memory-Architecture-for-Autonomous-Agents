A large language model is a function from text to text with no state between calls. An
\emph{agent} is what results when that function is asked to operate over days or months,
and the forgetting becomes the binding constraint. The standard remedy is an external
store: write experience to it, and on each turn retrieve a few items to place back in
context. The retrieval step is the subject of this paper.

Across the agent-memory literature---MemGPT \citep{packer2023memgpt}, MemoryBank
\citep{zhong2024memorybank}, A-MEM \citep{xu2025amem}, HippoRAG
\citep{gutierrez2024hipporag}---the sophistication is concentrated almost entirely in
\emph{what gets written and when it is evicted}. The read operator itself has been a frozen
cosine nearest-neighbour lookup for a decade. Three objections to it are standard. It gets
one shot at an ambiguous cue; it weights a memory recalled four hundred times identically to
one glimpsed once, patched by hand-tuned recency and usage heuristics bolted to the side of
a similarity score; and it can only return what is \emph{similar}, which is structurally the
wrong relation for recall by association---lightning does not resemble thunder.

A separate literature has studied exactly this operator for forty years. Associative memory
treats recall as a dynamical process: a cue is released into an energy landscape whose minima
are the stored patterns, and it descends until it settles \citep{hopfield1982}. Modern
variants hold exponentially many patterns \citep{krotov2016dense,ramsauer2021hopfield} and
repair a corrupted cue as a side effect of converging. The obvious experiment---use one as
an agent's persistent memory and compare---has not been run under matched conditions.

\paragraph{Why not.} We think the answer is Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}, and that it is
the reason this comparison has been quietly circular wherever it has been attempted. At the
operating point everyone actually uses, the two methods are the \emph{same operator}. One
step of the modern Hopfield update with the temperature turned up returns the top cosine
neighbour exactly. Dense retrieval is not a rival sitting outside the energy formulation; it
is a single corner of it. Any paper claiming that an energy-based memory beats similarity
search must therefore say which coordinate it moved, and by how far---and if it ran at high
$\beta$, it compared cosine against itself.

Stating the reduction first changes the question from \emph{``is energy-based memory
better?''}, which is not well posed, to \emph{``which departures from the dense corner pay
for themselves, and in which regime?''}, which is. It also makes every comparison in this
paper exact by construction: the baseline is our own code with different arguments, so there
is no implementation advantage to argue about and no encoder mismatch to suspect.

\paragraph{What we found.} Mostly that the departures do not pay, and---more usefully---why.
Of the mechanisms available beyond the corner, every one that changes only \emph{how an
answer is read off the landscape} fails on agent text: multi-step descent, residual peeling,
repulsive parallel descent, vote-count readout, multi-scale and per-memory $\beta$. The one
mechanism that helps does not touch the landscape at all; it is a model of \emph{what follows
what}, fitted on the agent's own transitions.

We began with a sign that appeared to flip between modalities, and a mechanism that
explained the flip. Energy descent computes a weighted average of stored patterns and moves
the retrieval state onto their span. That should pay off when the query is a degraded copy
of something already stored, because moving toward the memory manifold restores lost signal,
and should cost accuracy when the query is not, because averaging smears the discriminative
content of the cue. Question$\to$evidence retrieval violates the premise and descent loses
monotonically, as predicted. Image occlusion appeared to satisfy it, and descent won by more
as damage increased.

That positive half did not survive the three tests we nominated against it. Masking spans of
stored \emph{text} satisfies the premise and yields no $\beta$ that beats cosine in $45$
swept cells. A cue that is a degraded copy of \emph{two} stored images loses $-0.245$. And
the image result itself, re-examined, is not completion at all: its metric is ten-way
same-class accuracy, the cue's source image is never stored, and a soft $k$-NN class vote
beats descent at every occlusion level, while the premise's own best case --- occluded
copies of images that \emph{are} stored, scored by identity --- is a null
(\S\ref{sec:occlusion}). The premise now predicts every loss we measure and retains no
confirming instance. \S\ref{sec:taxonomy} withdraws the sufficiency half and the image
evidence rather than defending either.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item[\textbf{C1}] \textbf{The reduction.} Dense top-$k$ retrieval is exactly the
    $(T{=}1,\beta\!\to\!\infty,b{=}0,R{=}I)$ corner of the modern Hopfield update
    (Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}), verified at $1000/1000$ queries on synthetic vectors
    and $838/838$ on real sentence embeddings, both asserted in continuous integration. Every baseline in this paper is
    a configuration of the method under test. (\xid{X1})
  \item[\textbf{C2}] \textbf{The $\beta$ leverage band, and a hazard it implies.} $\beta$ has
    influence only within a narrow band, bounded below by collapse of the state to the corpus
    centroid on the first step and above by degeneration into cosine. Accuracy cannot see the
    upper edge---two retrievers can score identically while disagreeing about what they
    return---so we measure top-1 \emph{decision} agreement with cosine, which rises to
    $\mathbf{99.4\%}$ at $\beta{=}512$. A comparison run above the band therefore compares
    cosine against itself on nearly every query and will correctly report no difference for
    the wrong reason. The band is a property of the encoder, not a constant.
    (\xid{X3}, \xid{X11}, \S\ref{sec:band})
  \item[\textbf{C3}] \textbf{A regime taxonomy, and the withdrawal of its one positive
    instance.} Completion, composition and semantic match require mutually exclusive
    operating points, and the premise that separates them predicts a monotone $-0.084$
    across an interpolation sweep on $1{,}977$ \textsc{LoCoMo} questions. We then ran the
    three discriminating tests we had nominated against ourselves, and all three came back
    negative --- including a matched-baseline audit of our own flagship image result, which
    a soft $k$-NN class vote beats by $-0.036$ \ci{-0.043}{-0.028}. The premise is reported
    as \emph{necessary and not sufficient}, and the image result is withdrawn as evidence
    for an associative mechanism. (\S\ref{sec:taxonomy}, \S\ref{sec:occlusion},
    \xid{X113})
  \item[\textbf{C4}] \textbf{Eight diagnosed negative results.} Each is reported with the
    mechanism that produces it rather than as a bare null, including two that overturn claims
    we had previously made ourselves. (\S\ref{sec:negatives})
  \item[\textbf{C5}] \textbf{One externally validated positive, and a sharp limit on it.}
    A nonparametric predictor over the agent's own observed transitions beats cosine on
    one-hop temporal association, in three independent implementations on two corpora. On
    the public \textsc{LoCoMo} benchmark it raises Recall@1 from $0.064$ to $0.107$
    ($+0.043$, \ci{+0.036}{+0.050}, $p<10^{-4}$, $+67\%$ relative) over $5{,}233$ probes in
    $272$ sessions against a pooled $5{,}882$-turn bank. At \emph{two} hops the same
    experiment returns $-0.002$ \ci{-0.008}{+0.004}: association does not compose, and we
    withdraw a $+0.185$ two-hop figure of our own. (\S\ref{sec:composition}, \xid{X26})
  \item[\textbf{C6}] \textbf{The measured price of seven protocol shortcuts.} For each of seven
    errors we made, the magnitude of the false result it produced in our own data. Two were
    false \emph{negatives}, which proved far harder to find than the false positives. The
    seventh is the one we would most want read: an \emph{unmatched baseline} that every
    other safeguard in this paper was blind to, because split hygiene and pre-registration
    check how two arms are compared and not which two arms. (\S\ref{sec:protocol})
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What this paper is not.} It is not a system paper, and it does not claim a better
agent memory. The thesis we began with---that energy-based retrieval improves agent
recall---is refuted here by our own measurements, at adequate power, with the mechanism
identified. We think the resulting object is more useful to the field than the system would
have been: a reduction that makes the comparison exact, a taxonomy that says where descent
loses and that kept none of the positive instance we had claimed for it, and a quantified
account of how easy it is to publish the opposite conclusion.
