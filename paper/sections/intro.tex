A large language model is a function from text to text with no state between calls. An
\emph{agent} is what results when that function is asked to operate over days or months,
and the forgetting becomes the binding constraint. The standard remedy is an external
store: write experience to it, and on each turn retrieve a few items to place back in
context. The retrieval step is the subject of this paper.

Across the agent-memory literature---MemGPT \citep{packer2023memgpt}, MemoryBank
\citep{zhong2024memorybank}, A-MEM \citep{xu2025amem}, HippoRAG
\citep{gutierrez2024hipporag}---the sophistication is concentrated almost entirely in
\emph{what gets written and when it is evicted}. The read operator itself has been a frozen
cosine nearest-neighbour lookup for a decade. Three objections to it are standard. It gets
one shot at an ambiguous cue; it weights a memory recalled four hundred times identically to
one glimpsed once, patched by hand-tuned recency and usage heuristics bolted to the side of
a similarity score; and it can only return what is \emph{similar}, which is structurally the
wrong relation for recall by association---lightning does not resemble thunder.

A separate literature has studied exactly this operator for forty years. Associative memory
treats recall as a dynamical process: a cue is released into an energy landscape whose minima
are the stored patterns, and it descends until it settles \citep{hopfield1982}. Modern
variants hold exponentially many patterns \citep{krotov2016dense,ramsauer2021hopfield} and
repair a corrupted cue as a side effect of converging. The obvious experiment---use one as
an agent's persistent memory and compare---has not been run under matched conditions.

\paragraph{Why it is hard.} We think the answer is Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}. At the
operating point most systems actually use, the two methods are the \emph{same operator}.
We do not survey the literature to claim that prior comparisons were in fact circular --- that
would require re-running them, which we have not done. What we claim is narrower and
checkable: the hazard is structural, it is invisible unless the reduction is stated first,
and it caught \emph{us} (\S\ref{sec:protocol}, and the $\beta{=}512$ measurement of
\S\ref{sec:band}). One
step of the modern Hopfield update with the temperature turned up returns the top cosine
neighbour exactly. Dense retrieval is not a rival sitting outside the energy formulation; it
is a single corner of it. Any paper claiming that an energy-based memory beats similarity
search must therefore say which coordinate it moved, and by how far---and if it ran at high
$\beta$, it compared cosine against itself.

Stating the reduction first changes the question from \emph{``is energy-based memory
better?''}, which is not well posed, to \emph{``which departures from the dense corner pay
for themselves, and in which regime?''}, which is. It also makes every comparison in this
paper exact by construction: the baseline is our own code with different arguments, so there
is no implementation advantage to argue about and no encoder mismatch to suspect.

\paragraph{What we found.} Mostly that the departures do not pay, and---more usefully---why.
The departures split into two families and the split is the result. \textbf{Family~A}
changes the state update or the readout and leaves the landscape alone. We tested ten such
mechanisms --- multi-step descent, residual peeling, repulsive, noise-seeded and
cosine-seeded parallel descent, vote-count readout, multi-scale $\beta$, per-memory $\beta$,
query-conditional $\beta$ from the retrieval margin, and coupling the predictor into the
energy --- and none of them beats the dense corner on agent text. Table~\ref{tab:negatives}
is that list in full, with a diagnosed mechanism per row, and it is where the number ten
comes from. \textbf{Family~B} does not read an answer off the landscape, and both of its
members pay within stated limits. One is a model of \emph{what follows what}, fitted on
the agent's own transitions. The other changes the \emph{cue}: fold a few retrieved
memories back into the query and retrieve again, which pays on exactly those questions whose
second piece of evidence is reachable from the first rather than from the question, and pays
nothing anywhere else. Neither is attractor dynamics, which is the finding.

We began with a sign that appeared to flip between modalities, and a mechanism that
explained the flip. Energy descent computes a weighted average of stored patterns and moves
the retrieval state onto their span. That should pay off when the query is a degraded copy
of something already stored, because moving toward the memory manifold restores lost signal,
and should cost accuracy when the query is not, because averaging smears the discriminative
content of the cue. Question$\to$evidence retrieval violates the premise and descent loses
monotonically, as predicted. Image occlusion appeared to satisfy it, and descent won by more
as damage increased.

That positive half did not survive the three tests we nominated against it. Masking spans of
stored \emph{text} satisfies the premise and yields no $\beta$ that beats cosine in $45$
swept cells. A cue that is a degraded copy of \emph{two} stored images loses $-0.245$. And
the image result itself, re-examined, is not completion at all: its metric is ten-way
same-class accuracy, the cue's source image is never stored, and a soft $k$-NN class vote
beats descent at every occlusion level, while the premise's own best case --- occluded
copies of images that \emph{are} stored, scored by identity --- is a null
(\S\ref{sec:occlusion}). The premise now predicts every loss we measure and retains no
confirming instance. \S\ref{sec:taxonomy} withdraws the sufficiency half and the image
evidence rather than defending either.

Six further corpora then settled a question we could not answer from \textsc{LoCoMo} alone,
and they settled it against us in the useful direction. \emph{Completing a cue} from the
memory bank does not work in any regime we have found. \emph{Expanding a query} with
retrieved memories does, by $+0.023$ to $+0.171$, on exactly those multi-hop questions whose
second document is reachable from the first rather than from the question, and by nothing at
all on the two conversational-memory corpora this paper is about
(\S\ref{sec:corpora}).

\paragraph{Contributions.} Three scientific claims, then one methodological one.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item[\textbf{C1}] \textbf{The reduction, and what it does to fair comparison.} Dense
    retrieval is exactly the $(T{=}1,\beta\!\to\!\infty,b{=}0,R{=}I)$ corner of the modern
    Hopfield update (Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}), verified at $1000/1000$ queries on
    synthetic vectors and $838/838$ on real sentence embeddings, both asserted in continuous
    integration. The equality is exact at $k{=}1$; at $k>1$ the shortlist must be read off
    the \emph{cue}, because ranks $2..k$ of the converged state order patterns by proximity
    to the winner instead (Remark~\ref{rem:shortlist}) --- which is not a caveat but the
    mechanism behind the shortlist regression we report. Two consequences for anyone running
    this comparison. \emph{(a)} Every baseline in this paper is a configuration of the
    method under test, so there is no implementation advantage to argue about (\xid{X1}).
    \emph{(b)} $\beta$ has influence only within a narrow band, bounded below by collapse of
    the state to the corpus centroid and above by degeneration into cosine --- and accuracy
    cannot see the upper edge, since two retrievers can score identically while disagreeing
    about what they return. Measuring top-1 \emph{decision} agreement instead gives
    $\mathbf{99.4\%}$ at $\beta{=}512$: a comparison run above the band compares cosine
    against itself on nearly every query and will report no difference for the wrong reason.
    The band is a property of the encoder, not a constant. (\xid{X3}, \xid{X11},
    \S\ref{sec:band})

  \item[\textbf{C2}] \textbf{A systematic evaluation of associative retrieval dynamics, and
    the withdrawal of our own flagship result.} Family~A --- every tested mechanism that
    changes the state update or the readout and leaves the landscape alone --- is ten
    mechanisms deep and empty of wins (Table~\ref{tab:negatives}, one diagnosed mechanism
    per row, three of them overturning claims we had made ourselves). The organising premise
    is that associative completion needs the cue to be a degraded instance of a stored
    pattern; it predicts a monotone $-0.084$ across an interpolation sweep on $1{,}977$
    \textsc{LoCoMo} questions. We then ran the three discriminating tests we had nominated
    against ourselves and all three came back negative, including a matched-baseline audit
    of our own flagship image result, which a soft $k$-NN class vote beats by $-0.036$
    \ci{-0.043}{-0.028}. The premise is therefore reported as \emph{necessary and not
    sufficient}, and the image result is withdrawn as evidence for an associative mechanism.
    (\S\ref{sec:taxonomy}, \S\ref{sec:negatives}, \S\ref{sec:occlusion}, \xid{X113})

  \item[\textbf{C3}] \textbf{Two task-dependent positives, each with its boundary measured.}
    Family~B does not read an answer off the landscape, and both of its members pay.
    \emph{(a) Transition prediction.} A nonparametric predictor over the agent's own
    observed transitions beats cosine on one-hop temporal association in four measurements
    over three corpora: on \textsc{LoCoMo} it raises Recall@1 from $0.064$ to $0.107$
    ($+0.043$, \ci{+0.036}{+0.050}, $p<10^{-4}$, $+67\%$ relative) over $5{,}233$ probes in
    $272$ sessions against a pooled $5{,}882$-turn bank, and the same driver unmodified
    gives $+0.016$ \ci{+0.014}{+0.018} on \textsc{MSC} over $27{,}815$ probes against a
    $30{,}320$-turn bank. At \emph{two} hops it is $-0.002$ \ci{-0.008}{+0.004} and $+0.005$
    \ci{+0.003}{+0.006}, a factor of $18$ below the $+0.185$ two-hop figure of our own that
    we withdraw: association helps one step ahead and at best marginally thereafter.
    \emph{(b) Two-stage query expansion.} Folding $K_0$ retrieved memories back into the cue
    and retrieving again beats dense retrieval by $+0.023$ \ci{+0.010}{+0.037} on
    \textsc{HotpotQA} bridge questions and $+0.171$ \ci{+0.118}{+0.219} on \textsc{2Wiki}
    inference questions, surviving a disjoint sample, a second encoder and two bank sizes,
    with random modes losing by $+0.13$ to $+0.39$; on comparison questions in the same
    corpora selection declines to expand in $10/10$ folds. Those strata are benchmark
    annotations, so the figure we ask readers to quote is the unstratified one, with the
    label withheld from selection and scoring entirely: $+0.015$ \ci{+0.004}{+0.025} and
    $+0.043$ \ci{+0.029}{+0.056} over $n{=}2{,}000$ each. What predicts the gain is hop type,
    not target count --- our own pre-registered cardinality hypothesis comes out
    \emph{reversed} on four corpora (Table~\ref{tab:cardinality}) and we withdraw it. This
    is the one claim here that contradicts the thesis we began with; it is query expansion
    rather than attractor dynamics, and it does not transfer across corpora.
    (\S\ref{sec:composition}, \S\ref{sec:corpora}, \xid{X26}, \xid{X201},
    \xid{X202}--\xid{X204})
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{And one methodological contribution, which we do not rank beside the
three above but would most want read.} \textbf{The measured price of seven protocol
shortcuts:} for each of seven errors we made, the magnitude of the false result it produced
in our own data. Two were false \emph{negatives}, which proved far harder to find than the
false positives. The seventh is the one to take away --- an \emph{unmatched baseline}, which
every other safeguard in this paper was blind to, because split hygiene and pre-registration
check how two arms are compared and not which two arms. (\S\ref{sec:protocol})

\paragraph{What the taxonomy is, and is not.} We develop an empirical taxonomy of retrieval
regimes and characterise the conditions associated with success and failure in each. We do
\emph{not} demonstrate an automatic mechanism selector that generalises to unseen tasks. In
every experiment here the regime of a query is supplied by a benchmark annotation or by our
own construction of the cue; we built no inference-time detector and evaluate none, and we
decline to add one we could not evaluate under clean train/test separation. The taxonomy is
therefore a post hoc explanatory account of measured results, and an inference-time regime
detector is named as the next experiment rather than implied to exist (\S\ref{sec:taxonomy}).

\paragraph{What this paper is not.} It is not a system paper, and it does not claim a better
agent memory. The thesis we began with---that energy-based retrieval improves agent
recall---is refuted here by our own measurements, at adequate power, with the mechanism
identified. The one departure that does beat the corner (C3b) is query expansion on a
question shape we cannot yet detect without labels, it needs per-corpus tuning, and it is
null on conversational memory, so it is a finding rather than a recommendation. We think the
resulting object is more useful to the field than the system would have been: a reduction
that makes the comparison exact, a taxonomy that says where descent loses and that kept none
of the positive instance we had claimed for it, one sharply bounded positive that we did not
expect and did not want, and a quantified account of how easy it is to publish the opposite
conclusion.
