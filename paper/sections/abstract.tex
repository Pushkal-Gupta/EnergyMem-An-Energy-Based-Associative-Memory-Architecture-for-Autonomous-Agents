Agent long-term memory is almost universally retrieved by top-$k$ embedding similarity.
Associative-memory theory offers an alternative---recall as convergence to an attractor in
an energy landscape---but we are not aware of an evaluation of it as the \emph{persistent
memory layer} of an agent under matched conditions. We first show why such a comparison is
harder to run than it looks: at the standard operating point the two methods are the same
operator. Proposition~1
establishes that dense retrieval is exactly the $(T{=}1,\ \beta\!\to\!\infty,\
b{=}0,\ R{=}I)$ corner of the modern Hopfield update --- exactly at $k{=}1$, and for
$k>1$ only when the shortlist is read off the cue rather than off the converged state,
which is a distinction we measure rather than assume. It is verified numerically on
$1000/1000$ queries on synthetic vectors and $838/838$ on real sentence embeddings. The industry baseline is
therefore a configuration of our own code rather than a separate system, which removes
implementation advantage by construction and converts an unanswerable question into a
sharp one: \emph{which departures from that corner pay for themselves, and where?}

We answer it across three modalities and twelve corpora with matched encoders,
group-disjoint hyperparameter selection, and cluster-bootstrap intervals throughout:
$5{,}847$ held-out image queries, $1{,}977$ \textsc{LoCoMo} and $479$
\textsc{LongMemEval} questions, $5{,}435$ questions over five retrieval corpora, and
$63{,}763$ temporal-association probes across three corpora. The answer is negative for
all ten mechanisms we tested that change only \emph{how an answer is read off} a fixed
landscape, and the clearest evidence for that is the collapse of our own strongest positive
result.
Iterative descent beat cosine by $+0.014$ pooled \ci{+0.006}{+0.021} on occluded images,
rising to $+0.021$ at 70\% occlusion. That number reproduces exactly and means much less
than we claimed: the metric is ten-way same-class accuracy, the cue's source image is
never in the bank, and at the selected $\beta$ the first descent step averages $12.7$
patterns out of $400$ with half the blend mass on the cue's own class --- an implicit
class vote. Scored against the matched baseline that makes explicit, a soft
$k$-nearest-neighbour class vote with no energy, no iteration and no attractor, descent is
\emph{worse} at every occlusion level ($-0.036$ pooled, \ci{-0.043}{-0.028}). Building the
experiment our premise actually describes --- occluded copies of images that \emph{are}
stored, scored by identity rather than class --- leaves descent at $-0.003$
\ci{-0.008}{+0.000} against cosine. Descent loses monotonically on conversational
question$\to$evidence retrieval as well: every increment costs accuracy, from $0.336$ to
$0.252$ Coverage@5 across an interpolation sweep whose endpoint provably reproduces cosine.

One premise explains every loss. Associative completion requires the cue to be a degraded
instance of a stored pattern, and question answering violates it. We nominated the
discriminating tests against ourselves and ran all three: masked spans of stored
\emph{text} satisfy the premise and give no $\beta$ that beats cosine in $45$ swept cells;
a cue that is a degraded copy of \emph{two} stored images at once loses $-0.245$; and
occluded copies of single stored images, the premise's best case, are the null above.
The premise therefore predicts every loss we measure and retains \emph{no} confirming
instance --- we report it as necessary and not sufficient, and withdraw the image result
as evidence for an associative mechanism.

We further show that $\beta$---the only dial with leverage---is influential only inside a
narrow, encoder-specific band (${\approx}[32,64]$ on the vision backbone we measure it on), above which
descent degenerates into cosine --- at $\beta{=}512$ it returns cosine's own
memory on $99.4\%$ of queries. A comparison run above that band compares cosine against itself, which we
argue is a live hazard for this literature rather than a detail of ours.

The departures divide into two families, and conflating them is how this literature stays
confusing. \textbf{Family A} changes the state update or the readout and leaves the memory
landscape alone: we test \emph{ten} such mechanisms --- multi-step descent, residual peeling,
repulsive, noise-seeded and cosine-seeded parallel descent, vote-count readout, multi-scale
$\beta$, per-memory $\beta$, query-conditional $\beta$ from the retrieval margin, and
coupling the predictor into the energy functional --- and \emph{none} of them improves on the
dense corner under matched conditions, each failing with a diagnosed mechanism rather than a
bare null. \textbf{Family B} does not read an answer off the landscape at all, and both of
its members pay, within stated limits. The first is a nonparametric
predictor over the agent's own observed transitions, which beats cosine on one-hop temporal
association in four measurements over three corpora. On
\textsc{LoCoMo}, against a pooled bank of $5{,}882$ turns and over $5{,}233$ probes in $272$
sessions, it raises Recall@1 from $0.064$ to $0.107$ ($+0.043$, $[+0.036,+0.050]$,
$p<10^{-4}$; $+67\%$ relative) and Recall@5 from $0.174$ to $0.251$; the same driver, run
unmodified on \textsc{MSC}'s $30{,}320$-turn bank, gives $+0.016$ $[+0.014,+0.018]$ over
$27{,}815$ probes. The multi-hop extension does not survive: at two hops the effect is
$-0.002$ $[-0.008,+0.004]$ on \textsc{LoCoMo} and $+0.005$ $[+0.003,+0.006]$ on
\textsc{MSC}, a factor of $18$ below the $+0.185$ two-hop result of our own that we
withdraw.

The second member of Family B also pays, and we found it by running the six corpora that
\textsc{LoCoMo} alone could not speak for. Folding $K_0$ retrieved memories back into the
cue and retrieving again beats dense retrieval by $+0.023$ $[+0.010,+0.037]$ on
\textsc{HotpotQA} bridge questions and $+0.171$ $[+0.118,+0.219]$ on \textsc{2Wiki}
inference questions, survives a disjoint sample, a second encoder and two bank sizes, and
beats a random-mode control by $+0.13$ to $+0.39$ in $18$ of $18$ cells --- so the gain is in
\emph{which} memories enter the cue, not in expanding it. On comparison questions in the
same corpora, where both documents are reachable from the question directly, selection
declines to expand in $10$ of $10$ folds. Those strata are benchmark labels, so we also ran
the version with no label available at any stage: selecting and scoring on the unstratified
pool still gives $+0.015$ \ci{+0.004}{+0.025} and $+0.043$ \ci{+0.029}{+0.056} over
$n{=}2{,}000$ each, which we take to be the usable size of the effect. What predicts the gain is hop type, not target
count: our own pre-registered hypothesis that more targets should help more comes out
\emph{reversed} on four corpora and we withdraw it. The result is sharply bounded --- it is
query expansion rather than attractor dynamics, it gives up its entire gain when its
configuration is transferred across corpora, and on both conversational-memory corpora it is
null, where a lexical hybrid gains $+0.156$ $[+0.125,+0.189]$.

Finally, we report the measured magnitude of seven protocol errors we committed and
corrected. Each would have yielded a publishable but false result: a non-dimensionless
hyperparameter ($0.016$ vs.\ $0.277$ after normalisation), a pseudo-replicated bootstrap
($p{=}0.0005$ vs.\ $p{=}0.076$), test-set hyperparameter selection ($+0.042$ vs.\
$+0.004$), asymmetric candidate exclusion (an exact $0.000$ that read as a finding), an
unmatched step size ($-0.115$ vs.\ $-0.062$), and a significant result whose script was
never committed and does not reproduce ($+0.049$, $p{=}0.032$ vs.\ $+0.024$, $p{=}0.24$).
Two of the seven were false \emph{negatives}, which proved materially harder to find. We release all code, the regime taxonomy, and
an append-only experiment ledger in which every entry above appears with its date, its
question, and the entries it overturned.
